Does this machine ever halt?

1RB1LF_0RC0RB_0LD1RB_1LE0LB_0LA1LC_0LB---

Decided by an outside Lean proof: it never haltsAn open pull request (37) to DeepMind's cryptids repository proves it in Lean, and the proof was compiled here with only the standard axioms. It is their proof, on their definitions, not one written in this repository; the ledger below says what this repository checked on its own.

It never halts. The tape holds a binary counter at the left end and, to its right, a growing row of blocks; each pass of the head sends a burst of carries into the counter, and the burst doubles with every block. Busycoq pull request 103 proves that the burst is always big enough to clean up the counter, so the one bad pattern that would halt the machine never forms.

The machine

6 states, two symbols, 1 halting transition

Each entry says what to write, which way to move and which state to enter. The machine starts in A on a blank tape (all 0s).

The machine stops when it is in F reading 1. F is entered only from A1 (writes 1, moves L): 1 A>1. So a halt is the moment this pattern appears, with the head on the cell after >.

How the proof works

A counter that always gets enough carries

The proof is busycoq pull request 103 by Alex Havrilla of DeepMind: a hand-written Rocq file of 1,799 lines. It was compiled here with Coq 8.20.1 on busycoq's BB6 branch, in 4 seconds, and it uses no axioms (recorded in holdouts/external.json). Its main theorem is nonhalt : ~ halts tm c0.

Words. The proof reads the tape left of the head as a list of words 1o0a+1 (o ones, then a + 1 zeros, with o at most 3). Twenty-four macro steps say what the machine does to the first word or two of the list in each of three modes; each is checked by running the machine 2 to 6 steps. In three situations (state D just after one or two 0s, facing two or three 1s) no macro step is defined. These are the only roads to the halting transition, F reading a 1, so the proof has to show they never happen.

The shape. Write wn for the word w written n times. At the checkpoints the head is in state B on the blank cell right of everything, and the tape reads, left to right: a counter, a short body word, k blocks 1010100100, a fixed separator, (100)m, and 10. The counter is the left counter found on this page, with digits 1010 and 1000 and the lowest digit on the right. The proof reads it in bijective base 2: the digits stand for 1 and 2 instead of 0 and 1, so every number has exactly one way to be written and adding 1 is a carry running leftward. The invariant Inv284 lists 16 such shapes (16 body words), each with an inequality: the counter's value is at most a constant times (m + 3) · 2k.

The repeated step. In one pass the head runs left through the separator and the blocks to the counter and back, again and again. Each visit to the counter, a call, adds 1 to it, or removes a defect (below). Each block doubles the number of calls that get through it (call0N_repBlk), so a pass makes about m · 2k calls. At the end of the pass one or two new blocks are added (k grows) and m grows. The pass sometimes leaves a stray 10 in front of the counter, a defect. The lemma call1N_erase_0 proves that if the number of calls is at least about the counter's value (precisely 2length + 2·W, with W the value of the digits read as ordinary binary), the defect is absorbed and the counter is clean again. The inequality of the invariant says exactly that the next pass brings enough calls, and because k and m grow, the inequality still holds after the pass.

Why it never halts. From the blank tape, 1 machine step and 682 macro steps (step 2,210 of the machine) reach the first checkpoint, with m = 5, k = 0 and a counter of value 42, which satisfies its bound 16 ≤ 42 ≤ 48. Every one of the 16 shapes moves, in at least one macro step, to another of the 16 (inv284_step), so the undefined situations are never met and the machine runs for ever.

Computer and algebra. The arithmetic of the counter (bijective base 2, carries, the erasure lemma, a split of any digit string into four kinds of leading runs) is proved by induction and lia/nia, for every counter and every m and k. The computer only checks the 24 macro steps against the machine and the 682 opening macro steps (vm_compute). DeepMind's Lean cryptids repository also has an acceleration file for this machine (Accelerations/M_1RB1LF_0RC0RB_0LD1RB_1LE0LB_0LA1LC_0LBHHH.lean, a transducer); the Rocq proof stands on its own.

Structure

Two counters and two piles

Stop the machine each time the head stands on the leftmost cell in state F, and read the tape right of it in the words a = 1100, b = 110, c = 10. It reads

c^s  H  X_1 bb X_2 bb … bb X_w  b^m  cc0  b^j

  • c^s is a binary counter set to 0: its digits are four cells, 1010 for 0 and 1000 for 1, the lowest on the right. Lean proves how it counts: the carry runs left over the 1-digits, turning them into 0-digits, and sets the first 0-digit (incr); when every digit is 1 it runs off the end and the counter starts again at 0 with one digit more (overflow).
  • H is a short header, almost always ab.
  • X_1 … X_w is a second binary counter, with digit a for 1 and cc for 0, the lowest on the left.
  • b^m and b^j are two piles of the word b, separated by the marker cc0.

In one round the left counter runs through all its 2s/2 values. Between two of its increments the head runs right once and subtracts one from the second counter. Each time that counter passes from 0 to its largest value (a borrow), one b moves from the first pile to the second. At the end of the round the left counter overflows: the head reaches a new cell on the left in state F, and the counter is two cells longer (s + 2).

So a round sends t words from pile m to pile j, where t is the number of borrows in 2s/2 subtractions. In the next round there are twice as many subtractions, so t doubles: this is the doubling seen at the left end, (m, j) → (m − t, j + t) with t twice as large each round. All 39 such rounds up to 1010 steps follow it exactly (py/round_law.py).

Not modelled here

Phase changes

When the first pile is too small for the next round (m < t) the round ends differently. From the header ab the left counter comes back two digits shorter (s − 4) and the header becomes ababcc or abccbcc; from those headers the next visit has s + 2, the header ab again, one more digit in the second counter, and one more b in the piles. 24 phase changes happened up to 1010 steps; the new digits of the second counter are not yet predicted, so the run cannot yet be followed past them by arithmetic.

A halt needs F to read a 1, and a closed tape language of 5-cell windows confines it to F>1 1 1 0 1 0; the regular rounds never produce it, so the question was decided in the phase changes. The proof of pull request 103 (first section) covers them with its 16 shapes.

Halting

Where a halt could happen

The machine stops when F reads a 1. A closed tape language with windows of 5 cells, which contains every configuration reachable from blank tape, adds that the five cells right of the head must read 1 1 0 1 0: F>1 1 1 0 1 0.

The round law, on all 39 regular rounds up to 1010 steps

At the visits of the head, in state F, to the leftmost cell, the tape right of it reads, in words a = 1100, b = 110, c = 10, c^s H X_1 bb X_2 bb ... bb X_w b^m cc0 b^j, with a header H (ab, ababcc or abccbcc) and right-counter digits X = a (1) or cc (0), X_1 least significant, value v. With D = 2s/2 and t the number of borrows of D decrements from v (t = (D − v − 1) div 2w + 1), a round with m ≥ t (m > t when H is not ab) goes to (s + 2, H, v − D mod 2w, m − t, j + t). All 39 such rounds from step 1,756 to 1010 follow it exactly; within a phase t doubles, which is the doubling map (m, j) → (m − j − k, 2j + k) seen before.

The phase changes, and the question

When m is too small for the next round (m < t) the round ends differently: from H = ab the left counter loses two digits (s − 4) and the header becomes ababcc or abccbcc (four times it stays ab with m + j growing by 4); from those headers the next visit has s + 2, header ab, one more right digit and m + j + 1. The new digits are not yet predicted. Whether the machine halts was open here; busycoq pull request 103 (compiled here, no axioms; see above) proves that it never halts, phase changes included.
