Busy Beaver Explorer / holdouts / open problems

39 machines, 7 kinds of question

Which open problem does each holdout reduce to?

The number-theory ranking judged 39 holdouts to be genuinely Collatz-hard. For each one, Lean proves that the machine halts if and only if an explicit map does something specific. This page writes each of those questions down exactly: the map, the halting condition, how far it has been checked, a probabilistic guess, and the known problem it most resembles. None of the 39 is decided.

The 39 questions fall into seven kinds. Every one of them asks about one specific orbit of a map of the kind Conway showed can be undecidable in general. The strongest known results, like Tao's on Collatz, cover almost all starting values but never one given orbit.

  • kernel For 34 machines Lean proves "halts if and only if the map's condition is met", with no unproved assumption.
  • one way For 4, Lean proves the direction that matters for non-halting: if the condition is never met, the machine never halts.
  • partial For 1, level 1 (periods and the reset table) is now a Lean theorem, but the second level, where a halt could come from, is not.
  • heuristic 31 are expected never to halt. In each case the estimated chance of a later halt is small and shrinks as the orbit grows, down to 2-800,000. The 8 "epoch" machines are expected to halt eventually, but only after an epoch whose outcome no computation can reach.

The seven kinds of question

Landing on a sparse target

11 machines: ranks 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 14, 16

Does the orbit of an expanding piecewise-affine map ever land exactly on a sparse set, with about one target per doubling of scale (numbers like 5·2^k − k − 6)?

Heuristic. At scale N an orbit point hits a target with chance about c/N. The orbit grows geometrically, so the chances form a convergent geometric series dominated by its first unchecked term.

Related: The Collatz, Conway, Tao

Euclid's algorithm with multiplication

7 machines: ranks 3, 15, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22

Two counts play a subtractive Euclid game in which the smaller count is also multiplied (by 2 or 3) each round. Do the two counts ever come out equal, or off by one exact small constant?

Heuristic. The ratio of the two counts moves like a chaotic orbit of an expanding interval map, so the difference is about as large as the counts. Hitting one exact value has chance about 1/size per round, and the sizes grow geometrically.

Related: The Collatz, Conway, No residue obstruction

Powers of two in a 2-adic valuation sequence

5 machines: ranks 23, 24, 25, 26, 27

Write D = 2^v (2a + 1). The sequence D -> D + a + v + c (Katelyn Doucette's form) or a base-3 analogue. Does it ever reach a power of two (or of three)?

Heuristic. If the low digits of the terms are random, an n-bit term is a power of two with chance about 2^-n. The terms grow geometrically, so the total chance after the computed terms is negligible.

Related: Space Needle and Doucette's sequences, Erdős's ternary digits of 2^n, The Collatz

Antihydra-type walk with drift

4 machines: ranks 28, 29, 30, 31

A number is multiplied by about 3/2 or 4/3 each round, and a counter goes up or down by its last digit (parity, or residue mod 3). The counter drifts upward. Does it ever reach zero?

Heuristic. If the digits behave like fair coins or dice, the counter is a random walk with positive drift. From height h it ever returns to zero with chance ρ^h (gambler's ruin), and h is already in the thousands to hundreds of thousands.

Related: Mahler's 3/2 problem, Antihydra, Gambler's ruin, The Collatz

Nested counters and stacks

3 machines: ranks 2, 13, 32

A fast 3/2 counter drives a stack of slower counters, or a small counter must catch up with a large one. Does the whole stack ever empty, or the small counter ever reach the threshold?

Heuristic. The depth that the fast counter reaches into the stack falls off geometrically while the stack grows. Emptying it needs a run of coincidences, each about as unlikely as a Collatz hit.

Related: Mahler's 3/2 problem, Antihydra, The Collatz

Digit recursion in base 6

1 machine: ranks 17

A number is peeled digit by digit in base 6. Does the recursion ever end exactly on a repunit-type value (a block of trailing base-6 digits that runs off the end)?

Heuristic. For a block of D base-6 digits the halting pattern has chance about 6^-D, and D grows by a constant per round.

Related: Erdős's ternary digits of 2^n, The Collatz

Decision at the end of an exponentially long epoch (probably halts)

8 machines: ranks 33, 34, 35, 36, 37, 38, 39, 40

A 3/2 or 4/3 iteration runs for a precisely determined, exponentially long epoch. At its end one parity or residue decides between halting and a new, vastly longer epoch. Which residue comes up?

Heuristic. Each epoch end halts with a fixed chance p (1/5 to 3/4). So the machine halts with probability 1, but the next undecided epoch is far beyond any computation: deciding it needs one specific bit of an iterate with billions of digits or more.

Related: Mahler's 3/2 problem, Hydra, The Collatz

Run a map

The simpler maps can be iterated here, with exact big integers. Each row is one step of the map as Lean states it, and the last column shows how close the orbit is to its halting set. A machine halts exactly when its row says so. Starting from another value shows how sparse the halting set is.

Known problems these resemble

  • The Collatz (3x + 1) problem. Every orbit of a map like n -> n/2, 3n + 1 is conjectured to reach 1. This is open (Lagarias's surveys collect what is known). All of these machines ask the same kind of question about one specific orbit of a similar map.
  • Tao (2019): almost all Collatz orbits attain almost bounded values. The strongest known positive result is about almost all starting values, in logarithmic density. It says nothing about one specific orbit, and one specific orbit is what each machine asks about.
  • Conway (1972); Kurtz and Simon (2007): generalized Collatz maps are undecidable. Maps n -> a_i n + b_i, chosen by n mod m, can simulate any Turing machine. So whether such an orbit reaches a set is undecidable in general (Kurtz and Simon showed the universal form is Π⁰₂-complete). No uniform method exists, and each machine needs an argument of its own.
  • Mahler's 3/2 problem (Z-numbers, 1968). Is there a real ξ > 0 with frac(ξ(3/2)^n) < 1/2 for every n? This is open. Flatto, Lagarias and Pollington (1995) proved that the fractional parts of ξ(p/q)^n spread over an interval of length at least 1/p. The parities of the iterates of x -> ⌊3x/2⌋ are exactly such fractional-part events (Akiyama, Frougny and Sakarovitch, rational base 3/2 numeration, 2008). Controlling them along one orbit is beyond known methods.
  • Antihydra (bbchallenge cryptid). 1RB1RA_0LC1LE_1LD1LC_1LA0LB_1LF1RE_---0RA. It halts iff the walk x -> ⌊3x/2⌋, with a counter +2 on even and −1 on odd, ever goes below zero. The bbchallenge community treats it as the standard example of a Collatz-like cryptid. Its halting status is open, and a proof either way would be a result about the parity sequence of ⌊3x/2⌋.
  • Hydra (bbchallenge cryptid). Hydra is a cryptid of the same 3/2 kind as Antihydra. In it the counter must hit zero at the end of a long countdown. The 'probably halts' machines here share its shape: the outcome is a fair coin at the end of an exponentially long run.
  • Space Needle and Doucette's sequences. 1RB1LA_1LC0RE_1LF1LD_0RB0LA_1RC1RE_---0LD (Space Needle) halts iff D -> D + v + 3a (D = 2^v (2a+1)) from 6 ever reaches a power of two. Katelyn Doucette wrote the reduction in this form. The community has checked 10^8 terms. The other four machines here share the form with other constants.
  • Erdős's ternary digits of 2^n (1979). Erdős conjectured that 2^n has a digit 2 in base 3 for every n > 8. It is open, and it is the classic example of a question about the digits of an exponentially growing orbit in another base. The base-6 recursion and the power-of-two targets are questions of this type.
  • Gambler's ruin (the heuristic model). A random walk with steps +s_i and positive drift returns from height h with probability ρ^h, where ρ < 1 solves E[ρ^step] = 1. This is the source of the heuristic numbers on this page. It is a model, not a theorem about any machine.
  • No residue obstruction. For the subtract-and-multiply maps, the residues reachable modulo every 2^a 3^b up to 7,776 include the halting class (tools/number-theory/residues.py). So no congruence alone can prove non-halting; a proof must control sizes, not only residues.
