OpenNobody has proved whether this machine halts. Everything below is what is known so far.

Nobody knows whether this six-state, two-symbol Turing machine halts when started on a blank tape: it is one of the holdouts left open by the bbchallenge project. This page lists what has been established about it, sorted by what each result rests on.

Status as of 8 November 2026.

In short

The question is open. The macro structure is identified by simulation (rounds that halve every run of equal blocks); Lean proves the shift rules the sweeps are made of and that a halt can only come from 1 1 1 D> 0. In 109 steps D read a 0 about 21,000 times and every time the left neighbours were 1 1 0 or blank, one cell short of a halt.

The sweeps' shift rules

Moving right, A (with 1 0 behind it) and C (with 1 behind it) turn each pair 11 into 01, 6 steps a pair: a run of 2n ones becomes (01)n. Moving left, C (facing a 0) and D (facing a 1) turn each 01 back into 11, 2 steps a pair. B runs over 1s. Each holds for every n and every surrounding tape.

b_run, a_halve, c_halve, c_double, d_double, lean/Rules.lean

The shape of a halt

From 1 1 1 D> 0 the machine reaches F on a 1 in 3 steps and halts on the next. Conversely (by reading the table, not formalized) F is entered only from E on a 1, E only from A on a 1, and A has a 1 to its left only when it came from D on a 0 (from C or F it has a 0 there), so every halt comes this way.

halt_shape, halt_after_shape, lean/Rules.lean

heuristic Evidence only

Growth shape: exp/sqrt

Left of the start, the visited part of the tape grows like the logarithm of the step count: each new cell costs a roughly constant factor more time, the signature of a counter or of a Collatz-like map whose values are written on the tape (fitted exponent 0.113). Right of the start it grows like the square root of the step count, the signature of a head sweeping back and forth across the whole tape and adding a cell or a few at the end of each sweep (a bouncer-like shape) (fitted exponent 0.519).

tools/py/bbtools/triage.py

Rounds and the halving law

Sample the run when the head is in A at the left end of the tape (rounds; consecutive samples about 1.25 times further apart). At every sample the tape is a list of blocks 1k 00, almost all with k = 6j+1 and grouped in long runs of equal blocks, lowest level at the right. Between consecutive rounds a run of n blocks of level j becomes a run of ⌊n/2⌋−1 to ⌈n/2⌉+1 blocks of level j+1 (checked for every run of at least 10 blocks after the first 1.2·107 steps, to 1.6·109), and a new run of level-0 blocks 100 appears, alternately large and small (the large ones growing by about 1.25 each time: 1590, 2086, 2664, 3330, 4130, 5171). A few odd blocks (k = 5, 11, 17, ...) sit between the runs.

c/samples.c, py/rounds.py

Within a round: an expanding bouncer

After the left-end sample the head runs right to a point well inside the tape and then zig-zags in a window whose left edge creeps left and right edge creeps right (by a few cells per bounce, using the halving and doubling rules above), until the window reaches the left end: the next sample. This is where the square-root growth on the right comes from.

Does it halt from blank tape?

Not known. It halts exactly if D ever reads a 0 with three 1s on its left. That needs one of the odd blocks or a run boundary to line up badly during a round; the halving law looks like a binary counter on the run lengths with carries (the odd blocks), and nothing in the run so far approaches it. A proof would need the exact round map, including what happens to odd counts and to the junk between runs.
