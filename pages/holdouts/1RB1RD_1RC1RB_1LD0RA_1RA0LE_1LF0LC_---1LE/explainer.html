Decided: it haltsProved in this repository, checked by Lean and by Coq. The ledger below says what each part rests on.

Lean and Coq both prove that it halts, and Coq proves after exactly how many steps: a simulator that is proved to follow the machine runs it from blank tape to its halting transition. The machine keeps a binary odometer on its tape whose digits are fuel tanks; the tanks drain exponentially slowly, the odometer grows to 215 digits, and one day the carry runs off the left end of the tape. The simulator below runs the real machine, but it would need far longer than the age of the universe to get there.

The machine

6 states, two symbols, 1 halting transition

Each entry says what to write, which way to move and which state to enter. The machine starts in A on a blank tape (all 0s).

The machine stops when it is in F reading 0. F is entered only from E0 (writes 1, moves L): 0 E>0. So a halt is the moment this pattern appears, with the head on the cell after >.

Four levels

From six states to an odometer of fuel tanks

The question "does this machine halt, and when?" can be asked at four levels of description. Each level keeps less detail than the one above it and has a coarser unit of time, and each step down is a theorem saying that the lower level follows the machine exactly. At the bottom a program built from levels 2 to 4 runs from blank tape to the halting transition; that run is the proof (the bottom line).

Level 1 to level 2

The compressed tape

The first step down replaces the tape by a compressed tape: two stacks of items, one on each side of the head, where an item is a single cell or a word repeated n times, for any n. A round of the program (mstep in lean/Sim.lean) is one of four things:

  • a transition: the head reads the cell in front of it and the machine makes one step;
  • a run crossed: in front of the head lies a run of copies of a word, and one copy, entered in some state, is left on the far side in the same state. Then every copy is crossed in the same way, so the whole run is crossed at once and the steps are multiplied out;
  • two copies crossed: the same with two copies of the word taken together;
  • a copy unpacked: otherwise one copy is taken off the run and written out as cells.

Leftward moves are handled by mirroring the table, so one rule covers both directions (steps_mir). Kernel mstep_sound: every round leads from the configuration the stacks stand for to the next one by real steps of the machine, whatever is on the stacks. How the program folds cells into runs only affects its speed, never its correctness.

The stepper runs the program and the plain machine side by side. After every round the machine is moved on by the number of steps the round claims, and the two tapes are compared cell by cell.

Level 2 to level 3

One visit is a binary increment

Look at the tape each time the head, in state A, steps onto a fresh cell at the right end (a visit). Write n for the word 011 repeated n times. Then the tape is a list of counters with two-cell words between them, for example at visit 59 (step 10,094):

11 01 8 01 5 01 45 0 A>        that is  (011)^11 01 (011)^8 01 (011)^5 01 (011)^45 0

The words between counters are 11 or 01. Read 11 as the bit 0 and 01 as the bit 1: the tape carries a binary number, one bit between each pair of counters.

One visit adds 1 to that binary number, exactly as a car's odometer ticks: the bits at the right end that are 1 turn to 0, and the first 0 bit turns to 1. The head walks left only as far as that bit and comes back, so most visits are short.

The counters pay for it. The counter left of a bit loses one unit each time the bit is set (after the bookkeeping of the units that cleared bits pass along), and the last counter gains 2 at every visit. So the counter next to bit j is a tank that loses one unit every 2j visits: the last tanks drain fast, the first ones almost never. A visit that sets bit i takes 6(n0 + … + ni−1) + 4i + 11 steps.

Because the rule is this regular, any number of visits can be applied at once with a formula, as long as no tank runs low and the number does not overflow. That is what lets a program follow the machine for 10131 steps.

Kernel visit in lean/Zone.lean states this for every number of counters, every count and every tape left of the zone: if inc, the increment with its bookkeeping, applies to the bits and counts, the machine goes from the zone to the incremented zone. The proof follows the head: it walks left through the counters behind the 1 bits (state D turns each 011 into 100), turns at the 0 bit, and walks back (state C turns 100 into 011). inc gives no answer when every bit is 1 (an overflow) or a count that must pay is 0; those visits are the irregular ones.

Level 3 to level 4

Many visits at once: the tanks

The bookkeeping of a visit (a count loses one, a cleared bit moves one unit up) looks fiddly in the counts nj of the tape. In the coordinates

N_0 = n_0 − s_1,    N_j = n_j + s_j − s_(j+1),    N_k = n_k + s_k          (s_j the bits)

it becomes plain: at every visit N0 gains 2, and Nj−1 loses 1 each time bit j is set. Bit j is set once every 2j visits, so Nj−1 is a fuel tank that drains at that rate, and after V visits everything is known in closed form: bit j has seen about V/2j−1 increments, half of which set it (addV in lean/Jump.lean).

Kernel jump: if the bits do not overflow within V visits and every Nj (j ≥ 1) is still at least 2 after them, the machine goes from the zone to the zone after V visits. The proof is an induction on V: addV_succ says that one more increment after addV V is addV (V + 1), and inc_physP that the increment in these coordinates is the visit rule of the tape. The Coq proof adds the number of steps of V visits in closed form, a sum over the levels of the odometer.

The panel starts from the zone of visit 94,957 of the run, where the program jumps 16,383 visits in one go. Move V. The bars are the tanks; the lamps are the bits. The irregular part

Tanks that run dry, and overflows

When a tank is down to a few units, or when all bits are 1 and the carry runs past the last of them, the tidy picture breaks for a few visits: the words between counters become other short words (11101, 11101111, 1101101101, …), neighbouring counters merge or a new one splits off, and then an odometer is back with other contents. Nobody has a formula for these visits; the program runs them step by step on a compressed tape, where a run of a million copies of 011 is one item. There are 116,906 such visits in the whole run, between 86,729 formula jumps.

The first counter of the list is the slowest tank. It ran dry four times, near visits 105, 1014, 1039 and 1066. Three times the scrap it left at the left end turned back into a regular left end.

How it ends

The carry falls off the tape

The fourth time, the scrap was a different word, 11101101111101111, and each later overflow of the whole odometer ate a piece of it: 11101101111101, 11101101101, 1101101, 11111101111, 11111101, 11101111, 11101, 1111, 1. With a single 1 left, the tape was

The whole run, in your browser

The program of the proof, written again in JavaScript with exact big integers: the compressed tape of level 2, and at every visit the jump of level 4 when its conditions hold (the same choices as lean/Run.lean: counts of at least 7, a margin of 6, at least 8 visits). It counts steps as it goes, a jump with the closed form of the Coq proof. The second choice adds the two block rules of the Coq proof, which cross a whole counter level in one round. Either way it takes several seconds; the picture shows the odometer growing, one dot per jump.

rounds 0jumps 0visits 0steps 0counters 0time 0 s
Across: the number of visits, on a log scale up to 1066. Up: the number of counters in the zone the jump read; a zone is read only as far as its counts are at least 7, so many jumps see part of the odometer, and the upper edge is the whole of it. Every transition, run crossed, copy unpacked, block rule and jump counts as one round.
How sure is this

Proved in Coq and in Lean, step count included

The halt is a theorem checked by Lean. The proof has two parts. First, a small simulator written inside Lean is proved to follow the machine, whatever the tape: it keeps the tape compressed (a run of a million copies of 011 is one item), crosses a whole run when one copy is crossed in the same state, and at each visit may apply the odometer rule for many visits at once. That rule, and its formula for V visits, are proved for every odometer size. Second, Lean runs this simulator from blank tape: after about 89 million rounds it meets the halting transition. This last step is a compiled evaluation (native_decide), so it trusts Lean's compiler; everything else is checked by the kernel.

The same proof is also written in Coq, on the definitions of busycoq, the library in which the bbchallenge results are proved. There the run is evaluated by Coq's kernel virtual machine (vm_compute), so the Coq theorem does not trust a compiler. It is also faster, thanks to two more rules. When the odometer overflows, the head walks across every counter and back, and that walk is almost all of the work: 99% of the Lean simulator's rounds. A counter level looks like 1 0 (011)k 1 on the way left and (100)m 1 0 1 1 0 on the way back; the two rules cross such a level in one round, whatever k and m are, and are proved for every k and m. With them the run is 19 million rounds instead of 89 million, and takes under 3 minutes without counting steps.

The Lean proof shows that the machine halts, not after how many steps. The Coq proof also counts: every rule in it says exactly how many steps it takes, a visit of the odometer and a jump over many visits included, and the simulator adds them up as it runs. So the 132-digit number below is a theorem too, and being exact it is also the best possible lower and upper bound. It is the number the Python programs in the repository had computed: three simulators written separately, tested against a plain cell-by-cell simulator, give it as well. Counting makes the Coq run slower, about 8.5 minutes instead of under 3, because the formula for a jump multiplies numbers of a few hundred digits.

Kernel
visit, lean/Zone.lean
The visit rule of a zone, for every zone

When the tape ends in counters (011)nk … (011)n0 separated by 11 (bit 0) or 01 (bit 1), one visit is a binary increment of the bits: the lowest 0 bit si is set, ni loses 1, every bit cleared moves one unit to the counter on its left, n0 gains 2. Proved for every number of counters, every count and every tape left of the zone, by induction on the carry.

Kernel
jump, addV_succ, inc_physP, lean/Jump.lean
V visits at once

In the coordinates N0 = n0 − s1, Nj = nj + sj − sj+1 a visit is a plain binary increment in which Nj−1 loses 1 when bit j is set and N0 gains 2. For every V after which the bits have not overflowed and every Nj is still at least 2, the machine goes from the zone to the zone V visits later, given in closed form.

Kernel
mstep_sound, tryJump_sound, run_sound, lean/Sim.lean, lean/Run.lean
The simulator follows the machine

Each round of the run-length simulator (one transition, a run of a word crossed when one or two copies are crossed in the same state, a copy unpacked, or a jump whose hypotheses it has checked) takes the configuration it stands for to a configuration the machine reaches; if it answers true, the machine halts. The leftward half of the simulator is the mirror image of the rightward half (steps_mir).

Heuristic
README
The irregular visits

When a counter runs low or the bits overflow, the words between counters change (11101, 11101111, 1101101101, …) and counters merge or split; no closed rule is known for these visits, they are simulated (in the Lean proof too). The first counter ran out four times (near 105, 1014, 1039 and 1066 visits); three times the left end recovered, the fourth time it did not.
