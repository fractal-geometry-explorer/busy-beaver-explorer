Closing Gap Rule Map

Turing machine · 6 states, 2 symbols · one halt rule, out of reach for at least 4.8 × 10⁴⁸ steps

What each rule in 1RB0RE…0LD1RE does

From a blank tape this machine fills the tape with stripes, 1010…, and keeps a few marks in them: a gap of three 0s, markers of two 1s, and walls of two 0s. The head bounces between the gap and the next mark to its right. Each bounce moves the gap two cells right and a marker two cells left, so the two meet halfway. The eleven rules split into three ways of moving along the tape and a handful of short fixed sequences, and the mark the head reaches decides which one runs next.

Every routine works in pairs of cells. The stripes repeat every two cells, and each cycle covers exactly two: a carry cycle moves the packet two cells, a pass cycle crosses one 0 and one 1, and a shift cycle rewrites one of each. Anything that breaks the 1010 rhythm stops the cycle, and that is what makes the marks signposts.

One read in each cycle checks the tape. On the way right, E1 instead of E0 means a marker or a block, and F0 instead of F1 means a wall. On the way left, A0 instead of A1 means a gap, a wall or the blank end, and C1 instead of C0 means a marker, or a block.

  • The gap, 000. The head's home base. A carry that reaches it ends in a gap step, which moves the gap two cells right. Gaps left behind further left are asleep until a packet wakes them.
  • A marker, 11. A pass that reaches it ends in a marker turn, which moves the marker two cells toward the gap. A carry crosses it without moving it.
  • A wall, 00. A pass that reaches it turns back without moving it. A carry that reaches it turns it into a marker and a gap.
  • The packet, 111. Exists only while the head carries it. Each bounce makes one at the right and spends it on the gap at the left.
  • The ends of the tape. The blank tape on the right works like a wall. On the left it works like a gap: a packet that reaches it starts a new gap there.
  • A block, 111 at rest. Rare. A pass that reaches it shifts it away. A carry that reached it would halt the machine.

The big picture: halving

Every so often the tape is clean: nothing but stripes and the end's marker, 1 0 (10)ⁿ 1 1, while the head carries one last packet to the left end. The run below starts from the clean tape with n = 62 at step 43,385 and ends at the next one, n = 77, at step 79,549.

The packet starts a gap at the left end, and the gap closes in on the end's marker. Gap and marker move at the same speed, so they meet in the middle and swap. The gap goes on to the blank right end, which it can only reach two cells at a time, and the meeting there sends a packet back to start the next gap.

Each new gap first meets the marker the previous one left behind, at half the distance, and swaps with it. So the markers on the left sit at about 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/16 … of the way along. After the swap the gap goes on to meet whatever lies beyond: a marker (swap or merge), a wall, or the end.

After about log₂ n rounds the newest marker lands at the left end. The next packet to cross it turns it into a wall and starts a new gap just beyond. The gap meets the wall almost at once, and the shift runs over the whole tape to the right end. That clears the last marks and leaves a clean tape again, a few cells longer.

Every n is 2 more than a multiple of 3. Count the 1s on the tape, and add 1 when the head is in C, D, E or F, or 2 when it is in B. No rule changes this total mod 3. For example, A0 writes a 1 and switches to B, adding 1 + 2, and E1 erases a 1 and switches from E to B, adding −1 + 2 − 1. The blank tape starts at 0. When the last carry of a clean tape reaches the left end, the head is in A on 1 1 (10)ⁿ 1 1, which holds n + 4 ones. So n + 4 is always a multiple of 3, and n can only grow by multiples of 3: by 3 most often, and by as much as 57 in the first 3 billion steps. The next section works out the exact amount.

The marks as arithmetic

Everything the head does is decided by where the marks are, so the run can be followed without its steps. Keep a list of the marks and update it with a few rules. Each rule replaces thousands or millions of steps.

Count cells from the leftmost 1, which is cell 0, and let w be the number of cells up to the right end of the tape. A mark is named by its first cell: G g is a gap at cells g to g + 2, M p a marker at p and p + 1, W q a wall and B r a block. A round starts when a packet reaches the left end and opens a gap at cell 1. Right after the clean tape with n, the marks are G1 and M(2n + 4), and w = 2n + 6.

For a gap and the marker ahead of it, the number that matters is d = p − g − 3, the count of stripe cells between them. It is always even. Each bounce moves the gap two cells right and the marker two cells left, so d drops by 4 per bounce. What is left over at the end decides the meeting: 0 cells gives a swap and 2 cells a merge.

Odd n takes two rounds. The first gap starts at 1 and the end marker sits at 2n + 4, so d = 2n. For odd n that is 2 more than a multiple of 4, so they merge into a wall at 1 + n + 3 = n + 4. The new round shifts it to n + 6. In the second round the gap meets that wall, and the shift runs off the right end, where it adds an end marker and 2 cells. That leaves only stripes and the end marker, now at 2n + 8: the clean tape with n + 3.

Other n take longer. For even n the first meeting is a swap, and the marker lands in the middle at cell n. The next gap meets it with d = n − 2, so a multiple of 4 merges here and any other even n swaps again. Each further round halves the distance once more, so the outcome depends on more and more of the low binary digits of n, until a merge or a wall breaks the pattern. A multiple of 4 is settled in 3 rounds. n = 14 needs 24 rounds to reach 59, and n = 302 needs 23 to reach 341.

For 29 n in 32, the last five binary digits decide the next clean tape. Running the rules on whole residue classes of n gives the next clean tape exactly: n + 3 for odd n, n + 6 for n a multiple of 4, n + 9 for n that leave 6 or 10 when divided by 16, and n + 12 for n that leave 2 when divided by 32. Only n that leave 14, 18 or 30 when divided by 32 need more digits, and every right-end run of more than two meetings happens there. Each further digit settles about a quarter of what is still open.

Each rule is proved from the rule table. I ran the machine on each rule's starting pattern with the stripe lengths left as symbols and the tape around it unknown. So each rule holds for every length and whatever surrounds it, with an exact step count. A simulation that uses nothing but these rules, starting from a blank tape, reproduces every round and clean tape of the 3 billion step run.

The rules carry the run much further. That simulation reached step 16,470,069,173,330,669,103, about 1.6 × 10¹⁹, and the clean tape with n = 4,529,102, without meeting an arrangement of marks the rules don't cover. A fast program that applies the same rules to the list of marks then followed the machine's clean tapes to n = 14,874,910,607, roughly 6 × 10²⁹ steps. No two marks ever came closer than they already had by n = 302. The deepest cycles along the way, replayed with the certified simulation, give the same results.

Then by residue class. From there the jump to each next clean tape was looked up from the residue classes of n described in Does it ever halt?, and only the few n whose class is still open were simulated. Every millionth looked-up step was simulated again as a check, and all 103,032 checks agreed. That carried the clean tapes to n = 10¹², after 110,250,943,952 cycles and roughly 2 × 10³⁵ steps.

The clean tapes follow a fixed track. For almost every n the jump depends only on the last few dozen binary digits of n. So modulo 2ᵏ the clean tapes run around a fixed cycle of residues. Each lap of 3 · 2ᵏ visits a third of them, and consecutive laps share more than 99%. A residue class is either visited once per lap or never. That is why the 10-meeting cascades in the run come exactly 3 · 2²⁸ apart. The track also favours the residues where cascades start: along it, deep cascades are about 2.3 times as common as for n picked at random. That is no special property of the machine's own n: a third of its clean tapes are odd, and the rest land on residues with exactly the frequencies of a Markov chain on residues built from random n, which visits n that leave 2 when divided by 16 about three times as often as a random sequence would.

What keeps the right-end cascade going

Each meeting between a gap and the end marker moves the action near the right end about halfway closer to the end. Call the distance at a meeting d. The next distance follows from the binary digits of d + 2. If they end in 00, the meeting is a merge and d + 2 loses its last digit. If they end in 10, it is a swap: d + 2 loses its last digit and gains 5. On its own this rule would carry on to the end of the tape. What stops it is the supply of feeds from the left.

The feed is a halving clock at the left end. During a long cascade the leftmost mark is usually a marker that halves its cell number every round. Each round it swaps with the gap, the gap goes on to the middle and right of the tape, and roughly every second round that produces a meeting. When the marker merges into a wall near the left end, that run of fuel is over.

New fuel comes from blocks. Suppose a round starts with the gap facing a block at cell r. If r has one trailing zero in binary, the next round starts with a wall at r/2 + 4, which feeds the next round directly. If r has k ≥ 2 trailing zeros, it starts with a marker at r/2 that halves for k − 2 rounds. This held in all 4,471 cases checked. The first halving after a clean tape follows the same rule: the marker starts at 2(n + 2), swaps once for each trailing zero of n + 2, and merges into a wall at (n + 2 without its trailing zeros) + 4.

Each block forms at an average. On the main path, a block at r, with the wall near the right end at R and a tape w cells long, leads two rounds later to a block at (r + R + 11)/2, a right-end wall at (R + w + 11)/2 and a tape w + 6 cells long. The right wall's distance to the end goes from D to (D + 1)/2.

The simplest feed stops within 3 meetings. With only the gap, a wall near the left and a wall near the right on the tape, the right side needs d ≡ 2 (mod 4) to keep merging and the left needs the right wall at a multiple of 4. Each meeting moves that wall by d/2 + 7, so the two conditions clash by the second meeting. Every residue class checked ends within 3 meetings.

Every long cascade runs the same loop. Every cascade of 9 or more meetings that settles within 33 digits spends at least 4 of them in one 8-round loop, and all 256 with 13 or 14 meetings below 2⁴⁹ spend 7 to 9 there. The loop makes 4 meetings per pass. Its rounds start with three walls; a block and a wall; a halving marker and a block; the marker and two walls; the marker and a block; the marker and two walls; three walls; a block and a wall. Call the walls at the start of a pass a, b and c, and the end E. Another pass needs two things. E − c − 1 must have 4 more trailing zeros in binary: that is the end countdown above. And b must keep a fixed relation to the end, which in the limit is b = 16E/21 + 62/49 read as 2-adic numbers, to about 6 more binary digits. Counted exactly, each pass costs 10 binary digits, 2.5 per meeting.

Nothing cheaper keeps a cascade going. Starting from a block, a wall and an end in general position, I followed every branch of the rules exactly, splitting the possible positions in half at each test. From the sixth meeting on, the chance of reaching each further 4 meetings falls by exactly 1024, the cost of the loop. 20 million random starts give the same chances for the first 8 meetings. The loop itself has no built-in end: solving its conditions for 12 passes gives a block, wall and end that make 50 meetings in a row.

A clean tape can't hold the loop for long. On a clean tape every position depends on the one number n, so the two conditions become two targets for the binary digits of n. The left side, through its halving markers, reads about 1.5 new digits of n per meeting and the end countdown only 1. So the end only ever reads digits that the left side has already fixed. Once the left side is satisfied, whether the right end keeps merging is already decided, and the cascade lasts only as long as the two targets agree. For 100 cascades I fixed the digits that lead to the first meeting and followed every continuation for 40 to 45 more digits. Each has a longest possible cascade, reached by exactly one choice of digits, and no later digits go past it. All 256 cascades of 13 or 14 meetings enter the loop the same way, finish one or two passes, and leave when the end runs out of trailing zeros.

The loop is two countdowns, and one of them always runs out. Put R = E − c − 1 and L = 147b + 280E − 392c − 578. One pass turns R into exactly R/16 and L into exactly L/64, so the loop goes on while R keeps 4 and L keeps 6 trailing zeros per pass. L is always 3 more than a multiple of 7, so it is never 0, and it is at most 280E in size. So any one run of the loop makes at most log₆₄ 280E passes, about a sixth of the tape's binary length. Each pass brings the right end 16 times closer to the end, so the loop alone can cover at most about two thirds of the halvings a halt needs.

The two countdowns are one fixed point seen two ways. R = L = 0 is the fixed point of the pass, b = 16E/21 + 62/49 and c = E − 1. As ordinary numbers each pass pulls the walls toward it, so after a pass or two b sits near 16/21 of the way to the end on every tape. As 2-adic numbers each pass pushes away from it, so staying in the loop needs the walls ever closer to it in binary digits. L is the one integer that measures both, and it can't be 0. The card at the end of this section spells the argument out step by step.

Leaving the loop is short, and it never comes back. Starting from the loop's three-wall state with every position free, I followed every branch of one pass, with no limit on binary digits. The search is finite: 356 ways out. A pass completes with probability exactly 1/1024. Otherwise the run ends within 8 meetings and never returns to the loop. Every comparison it makes comes out the same for any spacing of the walls. So a run that reaches the loop makes 4 meetings per pass and at most 8 more.

There is a rarer second loop with the same lock. With one more wall near the end, the same 8 rounds can repeat. Each pass costs 14 binary digits instead of 10. Its left countdown is −441b + 735c − 2156d + 1652E − 179, again 3 more than a multiple of 7, so it is never 0 either. In fact the loop runs with any number k of extra walls, each costing 4 more binary digits per pass. Its pass map has a simple general form: the right walls are smoothed by 1, 4, 6, 4, 1 sixteenths each pass. For every k up to 2500 the left countdown's variable coefficients are multiples of 7 while its constant is 6·3ᵏ mod 7, never 0. Every loop in the family is locked the same way, though each lock only bounds one unbroken stretch: a run that switches pass types escapes it, as the hand-built tapes in Does it ever halt? show.

The binary-digit argument, step by step

1. Closeness in binary digits. Read a number's binary digits from the right. Two numbers are close in this sense, called 2-adic, when they agree in many of their last digits, that is, when their difference is divisible by a high power of 2. A number is 2-adically small when it has many trailing zeros. This has nothing to do with ordinary size: 2⁶⁰ is huge but 2-adically tiny, while 2⁶⁰ − 1, just as huge, is odd and so not 2-adically small at all.

2. A run at the right end reads binary digits. Each meeting between the gap and the end marker looks at the distance d modulo 4, which decides swap or merge, and then the distance roughly halves. Halving shifts the binary expansion right by one place, so the next meeting's test reads the next digit. A run of k meetings reads about k binary digits of the positions, from the bottom up, and a halt needs a single run that reads about log₂ of the tape width of them without breaking off.

3. Each pass of the loop uses up trailing zeros. In the loop's three-wall state the two countdowns are R = E − c − 1 and L = 147b + 280E − 392c − 578. One pass divides R by exactly 16 and L by exactly 64. Both must stay whole numbers, so p passes need R divisible by 16ᵖ and L by 64ᵖ: each pass spends 4 trailing zeros of R and 6 of L. When either runs out, the run breaks off.

4. L can't be 0. Its coefficients 147, 280 and 392 are multiples of 7, while 578 is 4 more than a multiple of 7. So L is always 3 more than a multiple of 7, on every tape, and never 0. Dividing by 64 keeps that remainder, since 64 is one more than 63 = 7 × 9. The 7 is no accident. L = 0 would mean sitting exactly on the loop's fixed point, and finding that point divides by 1 − 1/64, which brings in 63, so the point has 7s in its denominators (b = 16E/21 + 62/49). A wall sits on a whole cell, so no tape is ever exactly there.

5. Small, yet divisible by a large power of 64: the lock. A nonzero whole number divisible by 64ᵖ is at least 64ᵖ in size. But L is built from positions on the tape, so it is at most 280E. So 64ᵖ ≤ 280E, and one stretch of the loop makes at most log₆₄(280E) passes. At 4 meetings per pass that covers about two thirds of the halvings a halt needs. If L could be 0, nothing but R would stop the loop, and R alone allows all of them. That is why L ≠ 0 is the heart of the matter.

6. The same fact as a fixed point. As ordinary numbers, each pass pulls the walls toward that fixed point. In binary digits it pushes them away: staying in the loop means agreeing with the fixed point in 6 more binary digits per pass. L is the tape's offset from the point. As an ordinary number it shrinks 64-fold per pass, and in binary it must gain 6 trailing zeros per pass, and a whole number can't do both for long.

Seen on a real run. Take the closest approach found below 2⁵⁹, the clean tape n = 114,928,805,503,523,374, whose tape is about 2⁵⁷·⁷ cells wide. At three successive pass starts of its run, R has 12, 8 and 4 trailing zeros and L has 21, 15 and 9: exactly 4 and 6 fewer each pass. As ordinary numbers R goes from 2⁵³ to 2⁴⁹ to 2⁴⁵ and L from 2⁵⁸·⁷ to 2⁵²·⁷ to 2⁴⁶·⁷, 16 and 64 times smaller each pass, and L stays 3 more than a multiple of 7 throughout. Another full pass needs at least 5 trailing zeros of R, so after the third pass start the run leaves the loop, with L still holding 9. On this tape the end countdown ran out first, and the cycle got within 2⁻¹⁹·⁷ of the end, where a halt needs about 2⁻⁵⁷. All nine record runs up to 84 digits end the same way, with R down to 3 or 4 trailing zeros while L still has enough for another pass or runs out at the same time; L never gets near its size limit, so what stops them is the line-up, not the lock.

7. How many numbers you get to choose. On a tape with two free numbers, such as the marker position and the width of a two-marker state, both conditions can be met by choosing digits of both numbers: 10 digits per pass, 2.5 per halving. On the machine's own tapes everything depends on the one number n, and the two conditions become n ≡ a (mod 2⁴ᵖ) and n ≡ b (mod 2⁶ᵖ), with a and b fixed by the way the cycle came into the loop. Both can hold only if a and b already agree in their last 4p binary digits. That line-up is settled by digits of n read before the loop starts, so it can't be chosen as the loop goes. Each way in allows a fixed number of passes. Up to that number, passes are cheap, 6 digits each; beyond it, none.

8. Lucky lines, and why the odds come back to 2.5. The chart "The machine's tail is a staircase of lucky lines", further down, shows exactly this. A few ways in whose line-up happens to be long carry the machine's near misses at each depth, each at 1.5 digits per halving, and then stop. A line-up of g digits is about as likely as a random number having g trailing zeros, 1 in 2ᵍ, so going deeper costs another digit per halving: 2.5 again on average. That is measured, not assumed: the line-up of a way in can be read straight off its two countdowns by solving congruences, and over 693,518 of the machine's own lines each extra loop meeting a line allows is rarer by 0.85 to 0.99 binary digits, out to 20 meetings. That average gives the odds quoted below, and the closest approaches found stay about a third of the way to a halt.

9. Why this is not yet a proof. Every step above is exact except one: that line-ups keep behaving like the trailing zeros of random numbers. That is measured out to 20 loop meetings, but a halt needs about as many meetings as n has binary digits, and there are infinitely many ways into the loop, each with its own line-up. To rule out that one of them has an enormous run of trailing zeros is a statement about the binary digits of particular whole numbers. Such statements are easy to weigh and, so far, impossible to prove, which is the situation of the Collatz problem.

Does it ever halt?

In brief

  • No halt below n = 2⁶¹. Every clean tape below 2⁶¹ was checked through its residue class, so the machine runs at least 1.8 × 10⁵² steps without halting.
  • A halt needs one long run at the right end: about log₂ n meetings in a row. The longest anywhere below 2⁶¹ is 19.
  • Long runs are one loop, and it is locked. One stretch of it covers at most about two thirds of what a halt needs, and it ends the run. Its ending is now known exactly: the run can reach the end only from at most 176 cells away, with the left countdown in a fixed residue class. So a halt needs the loop to start late, and beyond the checked range later than any run seen by a wide margin.
  • The usual ways into that loop can never lead to a halt. For every way in found within 62 binary digits, which covers all but about eight in ten billion two-marker states, the exact end of the loop alone rules out a halt on any tape wider than 2⁵⁹·² cells, and the checked range covers the rest. The commonest way can't lead to a halt above 2²²·⁴ cells at all.
  • The rules alone do allow a halt. Hand-built tapes with three free numbers halt at every width tried up to 2²⁰⁰: a stretch of a rarer loop, a drop into the locked one, then a stretch of it.
  • The machine's own tapes have too few free numbers. They have one (two for the wider family they sit in), while a halt costs about 2.8 binary conditions per halving. The expected number of halts beyond 2⁵⁵ comes out at 1 in 10²⁴ or less. The machine's own tail looks cheaper for a while, but that stretch is a few lucky lines of states, and lines that line up further are rarer by a binary digit per halving.
  • And the rarer loop is capped outright for them. Each loop has a number of countdowns, two for the locked loop and three for the rarer one, and a family of tapes can follow a loop without a fixed limit only if it has at least that many free numbers. So for the machine's tapes the rarer loop stops after a fixed number of passes for each way in. For the commonest ways in that number is zero or one; among 190,923 ways in built from all routes to 30 binary digits, 81 allow two passes, 8 allow three and none allows four.
  • The closest any clean tape is known to come is about a third of the way. The best found reach 19.7 halvings at 57 binary digits and 29.6 at 94, where a halt needs about 57 and 94.
  • No proof yet. What is left is a statement about binary digits of the same kind as the Collatz problem. The section What is established, and how firmly grades every statement on this page and states the main theorem in full.

D1 is the only missing rule, so the question is whether D ever reads a 1. D runs after F0 or C1. After F0 it always lands on the 0 that E wrote two steps before, as in every wall turn. C runs only after B0, and tracing back from there, D can read a 1 in just one way: a carry cycle starting with A1 E1 when the two cells ahead of its packet both hold 1. In the stripes the first of those is a 0, which gives C0 and the carry goes on. At a marker the first is a 1 and the second a 0, which gives C1 D0, the marker pass. Two 1s in a row ahead of the packet needs a block, 111, or a longer run of 1s.

Blocks do form: a shift that runs into a wall turns the wall into one. But a shift stops at the first mark it meets, so the stretch just left of a new block is plain stripes. The next time the head arrives there it is passing right from a gap, and the pass shifts the block away. In the first 3 billion steps a pass reached a block 68 times, and no carry ever did.

The rules in The marks as arithmetic make this exact. Within them a block always lies to the right of the gap or the packet: a block appears only where a shift stops, the packet then heads left from it, and the next pass or shift to arrive removes it. So within the rules a carry never reaches a block.

That still isn't a proof that the machine never halts. The rules assume the marks never touch in a few odd ways, and the ones that lead to a halt end the same way: a new marker is made directly against a 1, fuses with it into a block left of the head, and the next carry halts on it. That happens when a carry reaches a wall with a marker directly before it or exactly two stripe cells after it, and also when a long run's last meeting is a swap right at the end, as in the hand-built tapes below.

The only route to either that I have found needs the activity at the right end to halve its distance to the end about log₂ n times in a row: a wall near the end is turned into a block, passed, and remade closer to the end, once for each meeting between a gap and the end marker. That run of meetings is short, as What keeps the right-end cascade going shows. The longest below 2⁶¹ is 19 meetings, reached only by rare single clean tapes, and along the machine's own clean tapes, followed to n = 10¹², the longest is 14.

On a tape built by hand, that route really does halt the machine. I set the loop's two countdowns by hand on a 72-cell tape: gap at 1, walls at 7, 30 and 39, head on the gap in state E, tape 100010100101010101010101010101001010101001010101010101010101010101010101. The meetings at the end run 30, 14, 6, 2, 0, and the machine halts after 10,302 steps: state D reads the first 1 of a 111 next to the end. A 16,000-cell version follows the mark rules exactly for 17 rounds and then halts at step 759,437,834. On a 17,652-cell tape the last wall can start 8,192 cells from the end, mid-tape, and the machine halts at step 1,258,643,838. I also ran every three-wall round start of width 40 to 130, 423,775 of them, on the real machine. It halted only when the mark rules' right-end run got within 2 cells of the end, or when marks touched. Every clean tape below 5,000 run directly on the machine reaches the next one. Nothing suggests these tapes can be reached from blank. The lock keeps the plain loop from doing this on big tapes: from about 2¹⁷ cells on, one run of the loop can't start from mid-tape, and the machine's own tapes are now longer than 2⁵⁶ cells. That extrapolates tails measured out to about 20 halvings, so it is an estimate, not a proof. The rules also assume the marks never touch in the ways described above, which every certified cycle bears out but which is not proved for all n.

A halt would need a run that enters the loop late. A run's first meeting is usually within about 4 halvings of the full width, however close the run before it got (29 million runs checked), and every run seen is a short lead-in, at most one stretch of the loop and a short exit (147 million runs; among 5.8 billion runs from random 60-bit cycles, 3 million contain a stretch of the loop and none contains two). That alone caps a run at about two thirds of log₂ n plus a constant. Counted run by run it is sharper. At a run's first pass start, say the end is 2⁻ˢ of the width W away and L is 2^λ times W. A pass divides R by 16 and L by 64, so 3s + 2λ stays the same at every pass start of the stretch, and what matters is the last one. I searched the end of the loop exactly: from a pass start with the end distance R fixed and the other positions free, every way the run can go, for every even R up to 4096, in two independent implementations, one of which decides every comparison of positions symbolically and reports when it cannot (it never had to). The run gets within 2 cells of the end marker only from R = 2, 4, 8, 16, 24, 32, 48, 64 or 176, and each of these needs L in a fixed residue class. For R = 176, L must be a multiple of 128, so it is at least 256 in size. Together that proves (within the mark rules) that a run that halts through the loop has 3s + 2λ of at least log₂W − 6.4. Counting halvings alone had given log₂W − 24. Over a million loop entries, in random two-marker states, in the deepest classes of the exhaustive check and in the record cycles, 3s + 2λ never exceeds 32.2, which allows a halt only on tapes narrower than 2³⁹. The latest of those entries come from a staircase of short runs, a 7-round pattern that pushes run starts out to 5.6 halvings. Its own affine map pulls the last wall toward a fixed point 0.0084 of the width from the end, so like the loop it can't creep up on the end. It can still make the next run enter the loop late: from a staircase run all the way to its limit, exactly 1/32 of the ways out enter at 3s + 2λ = 36.4 (every branch from the limit followed, to 40 binary digits), and that is the latest exit from any of the 118 plateau limits that uses no round of the extra-wall loop; it allows a halt only below width 2⁴³, far inside the checked range. Exits that do pass through extra-wall rounds reach 47.3 from those limits, at measures below 10⁻¹¹, and the route tree's deep lead-ins reach 52.9. A halt beyond the checked range (tapes up to 2⁶² cells) through the loop would need 3s + 2λ ≥ 55.6, which means entering the loop at least 12 halvings below the width. The rest of the way would have to come from the extra-wall loop. Following every branch from an extra-wall state shows there is no ceiling: each meeting a run makes before it enters the plain loop adds 3 to 3s + 2λ and costs about 3.4 binary digits, against 2.5 per halving inside the loop. Among the machine's own two-marker segments, chains through the extra-wall loop found within 46 binary digits already enter 18.9 halvings below the width; all of them were checked and none halts. So the lock doesn't close the gap on its own. What it does is make about a third of the halvings a halt needs cost 3.4 digits instead of 2.5.

The rarer loops leave a gap. In the loop with an extra wall, a pass whose halving marker runs two rounds longer works as a detour. The pattern can resume after it, or the run can drop the extra wall and carry on in the plain loop. Each new stretch starts with a fresh left countdown, about 50 times E in size, while the right end keeps closing in. So the lock limits each stretch but not a run made of several. These chains are no cheaper: each further 4 meetings still costs 10 binary digits. In clean cycles the extra-wall states have a share of only about 4 in 10⁸. Among every residue class settled within 40 digits, 631 runs use the extra-wall loop, all of them short, and no run contains two stretches of any loop. A proof would need to rule chains out some other way, and the hand-built tapes below show that the rules alone can't.

Why one loop is locked and a mixture isn't. A pattern of rounds that repeats is an affine map, and over the ordinary numbers it pulls the state toward its fixed point. That point is a fraction with a fixed denominator, and when it isn't a whole-number state, the state's distance to it is a nonzero multiple of 1/D that shrinks every pass. So the pattern can only repeat a bounded number of times. The mod-7 lock is this, with the 7 coming from 63 = 64 − 1. The detour is a second 8-round pass whose map has irrational eigenvalues, (13 ± √153)/256. So no linear countdown locks the detour, but a quadratic one does: the product of its two eigenforms is an integer quadratic, never 0, that every detour divides by 4096, so a stretch of detours, like the plain loop, covers at most two thirds of the halvings. Every repeating word of standard passes and detours has a lock of this kind (checked for all 747 words up to length 12), and none covers more than 4/5 of the halvings. Each different order of standard passes and detours has its own fixed point, with its own denominator, so a long mixture has no single point to be held away from, and no lock for every mixture can exist: the hand-built tapes below halt at every width by a mixture. What the locks still say is that such a halt has to change words at least once, and each change starts a fresh lock. Such mixtures cost 16 to 18 binary digits per pass, against 10 for the plain loop.

Every other repeating pattern parks short of the end. I scanned the machine's runs for any sequence of rounds that repeats, in 4 × 10⁸ random two-marker states and about 200,000 of the machine's own cycles, and found 125 kinds. For each I read off its affine map and the point it pulls the marks toward. Only the plain loop and two-round pieces of the plain and extra-wall loops pull a mark onto the end; the pieces never repeat more than three times, and the extra-wall loop never ran two whole passes in these samples (its own pass map also pulls a mark onto the end, and it has a lock too). The other 118 settle a fixed fraction of the width short of the end, at most 7.9 halvings away, the staircase being the latest. So the only repetitions that can carry the right end toward a halt are the ones with a lock.

So a proof has to use where the tapes come from. Nothing in the rules stops a long run. What has kept every run short so far is that the machine's own tapes are not chosen freely. Each run starts from a family with a single free number, n, and a long run needs that one number to satisfy about two and a half binary conditions per meeting. A tape built by hand has four free positions, enough to satisfy them all. Nor does a conserved count rule these tapes out: they pass the mod-3 count of 1s, and a search for other counts of the same kind, weighting each cell by its value and its position modulo up to 6 and the head by its state, with moduli 2, 3, 5 and 7, finds none.

A halt needs at least three free numbers; the machine's runs have two. One stretch of the plain loop covers at most about two thirds of the meetings a halt needs, and it always ends the run. So at least the first third has to come from a pattern the lock does not cover: the extra-wall loop, at 14 binary conditions per 4 meetings, then a drop into the plain loop that keeps the progress made, then the plain stretch at 10 per 4. That averages 17/6, about 2.83 conditions per meeting. A family of tapes with d free numbers at width W can meet about d·log₂W conditions, and a halt needs about log₂W meetings, so it needs d above 2.83: at least three free numbers. Three do suffice. With one combination of the hand-built tapes' walls held fixed, so that three free numbers remain, the same lattice method finds a 2¹²⁰-cell tape on which the machine halts after ten extra-wall passes, a drop and eighteen plain passes, and tapes that halt the same way at every width tried, 2²⁴, 2³², 2⁴⁰, 2⁴⁸, 2⁶⁴, 2⁸⁰, 2⁹⁶, 2¹²⁸, 2¹⁶⁰ and 2²⁰⁰, while words made only of extra-wall passes and detours find nothing in that family at 2¹²⁰. On a 129,963-cell tape built the same way (one extra-wall pass, the drop, two plain passes), the bare transition table halts at step 75,047,708,436, exactly as the rules predict. The drops are picky: right after an extra-wall pass the simplest one is impossible, and most of the others leave the last wall between a half and a seventeenth of the tape from the end, losing all the progress. And every cycle from an even clean tape is, one round in, at a state with just the gap, one marker and the end marker, so everything after depends on two numbers (really one, n). And for such states the extra-wall loop is capped outright, not just made unlikely. Each of its passes has to keep three countdowns divisible by growing powers of 2, where the plain loop has two. A family with three free numbers can do that for as many passes as its size allows; in a two-number family the third countdown is tied to the other two by a fixed number, which then needs four more binary digits to line up for every pass. Random two-number families never allowed two passes in my tests, random three-number families allowed every word of five, and the 144 ways from a two-marker state into that loop within 30 binary digits allow at most one pass, decided by their position alone. Rarer ways in allow more, each extra pass about ten times rarer than the last: there are two-marker states about 2³⁵ cells wide whose segments run three extra-wall passes in a row, and they still come no closer to the end than 2⁻¹⁵ of the width. Even a chain that keeps its progress falls further behind as it goes: after two extra-wall passes and a drop, each further plain pass needs about 5 more binary digits of width before any two-marker state follows it, and buys only 4 halvings; the first state that adds 8 plain passes is 2⁷⁷ cells wide and stops 38 halvings short. Two-marker states in general have odds of ending within 2⁻ʰ of the width that fall by about 2.5 binary digits for each extra halving, at every width, and the machine's own tapes, a one-number line inside that family, have odds 20 to 30 times smaller still.

None of this depends on where the marks sit. Every rule places a new mark at the midpoint of two neighbouring marks, give or take a few cells, or moves marks by a few cells. Measure each mark by its distances to the marks that started out on the tape: if two neighbours are ordered in every one of those measures, so is their midpoint. So the left-to-right order of the marks is the same whatever the starting distances are, as long as they are large. For a two-marker state that means the whole tree of cases is the same wherever the marker sits; built at nine positions from 1/64 to 63/64 of the width, it came out identical, and none of its 250 million comparisons of two positions depends on the ratio. The same fact settles a loose end in the earlier analysis: the searches that fixed one geometry for convenience give the same answer for every geometry. It also bears on the touching marks the rules can't handle. Along every branch decided within 42 binary digits, the only two neighbouring marks that ever sit a fixed distance apart, rather than a distance that grows with the tape, are the end marker and the end itself: of 69 million neighbouring pairs checked, 13 million are that pair and no other pair ever is. So on a large tape, marks can touch only on a branch that needs more digits than that.

Every halt would have to start from a two-marker state. A cycle reaches its first two-marker state after one round (odd n go straight to the next clean tape in two fixed rounds), and from then on it is a chain of segments, each depending only on where its marker and end marker sit. So from any clean tape the machine can halt only by reaching a two-marker state whose own segment halts, a question about one fixed two-number family rather than about the states the machine happens to visit. I ran every two-marker state up to 262,143 cells wide through its segment, 8.6 billion of them, but left out the marker's last position, three cells before the end marker: only 7, all between 11 and 29 cells wide, get within 2 cells of the end or let marks touch, and on the real machine exactly one of those halts. It is 13 cells wide with the marker touching the gap, and its end meetings run 6, 2, 0. The last position holds two more halting states, 9 and 17 cells wide; at every other width up to 262,143 it reaches the clean tape, and the other narrowest gaps add no new halt. No cycle passes through any of the three: the narrowest two-marker state the machine reaches is 15 cells wide, and its segment leads to one 27 cells wide. The odds measured on random two-marker states predict about one halting state below 2¹⁶ (there are three, all tiny) and about 0.014 beyond 2¹⁸. For the commonest way into the loop, the one 230 of the 256 longest cascades below 2⁴⁹ take, the question can be settled at every size. That route always starts at a two-marker state, and a halt through it needs both of its countdowns to be divisible by large powers of 2, which pins the width to a residue of −38423/1225 and then the marker to one residue. Counted with two halvings to spare, that leaves 332,846 candidate states between 2¹⁷ and 2⁶¹ and none beyond, since the lock forbids it there. None of them halts, so within the mark rules no cycle can ever halt through that route. The best candidate, about 2⁴⁶ cells wide, runs the loop for 8 passes and gets to 1,030 cells from the end, within 2⁻³⁶ of the width, before its left countdown runs out, much closer than the machine's own record of about 2⁻¹⁵: that is what a second free number buys. The same argument works for any fixed way into the loop. Following every branch of a two-marker segment exactly gives 759,918 ways in that are decided within 46 binary digits (2,767 within 30). The machine's own states start out special. A cycle is a chain of segments, each running from one two-marker state to the next or to the clean tape, and each segment ends in another two-marker state with odds of about a third. Starting from a random two-marker state, any segment gets within 2⁻⁴ of the width with odds of about 1.5 in 1,000. On the machine's own tapes the first segment never does: following every residue class through it proves that its meetings stay at least 2−1.6 of the width from the end. The second segment does so about 3 times in 10 million, and the odds then climb, reaching the random level only by the seventh to ninth segment. The reason is how few kinds of state come first. With the marker at cell m and the width E, the first two-marker state of every cycle lies on the line E = 2m + 7; the second lies on one of just two lines, E = 8m + 13 or 13E = 16m + 127; the third on dozens, the fourth on hundreds. Each return also brings about 3.5 new binary digits of n into the marker's position, while the width's low digits stay the few that the history allows. The record 14-meeting cycle below 2⁴⁹ has its long run in its eighth segment. The same returns are what make the exhaustive check expensive: the digits a class needs grow by about 3.5 per return, whether or not the cycle comes close to the end.

On the machine's own tapes, each gap is a count of binary zeros. There every position is λN + κ with N = 2n + 6, and λ and κ are fractions over the same power of 2. At a loop pass start, the difference between the two countdowns' targets, scaled by their slopes, is a number Δ: in all 878 pass starts reached from long-cascade clean tapes it is below 800. Each gap is the number of trailing zeros Δ has when written to as many binary places as λ. So for one stretch to finish the job, Δ must be a whole number, or nearly, with about as many trailing binary zeros as n still has unread digits at the pass start, give or take the few halvings the exit adds. With Δ below 800, that allows at most about 16 unread digits. The 256 longest cascades below 2⁴⁹ reach their first pass start with 2 to 19 digits unread, but their Δ's are far from whole: written out in binary they run to within 8 places of λ's, some 25 places past the point. Ruling out such a Δ for every n is a statement about binary digits of the same kind as the open questions about Collatz-type maps, where the odds are easy to estimate and no proof method is known.

The commonest way into the loop has a ceiling. Of all pass starts on the machine's tapes up to 38 binary digits, 90% come in by the same four rounds: a halving marker and the end marker, then three markers, then two markers and a wall, then a marker and two walls. For those the gap has a closed form, and it depends only on the tape's width W at the first of the four rounds: it is 3 less than the number of binary digits in which W agrees with the 2-adic number −38423/1225. (Every mark's line passes through the same 2-adic point, W itself, to as many digits as the class has read, and the halving marker's slope then cancels out.) On every such line up to 39 digits, all 308,333 of them, W agrees in at least 5 digits and never in 12, so this route's gap never exceeds 8. The route occurs only at particular stages of the cycle, each with a fixed growth g of the width since the clean tape: g = 23, 95, 119, 191, 215, 287 and 383 among all those found, with a search that follows the longest-lived residue classes out to 80 binary digits. At each stage the low digits a clean tape needs in order to get there stay a few places away from the digits of the 2-adic number (−38423/1225 − 6 − g)/2, and the best stage's gap is still 8. 230 of the 256 longest cascades below 2⁴⁹ come in this way, all at exactly that ceiling, which is why they stop after one or two passes. The larger gaps, up to 12 by 43 digits, come from rarer routes.

What is established, and how firmly

Four grades of statement appear on this page, and it matters which is which. proved means derived from the rule table and holding for every size, by symbolic runs or by a finite search that covers every branch. checked means a finite computation that covers every case up to a stated bound, and nothing beyond it. measured means seen in every case examined, sampled or exhaustive, with no argument that it continues. heuristic means odds estimated from the measured prices. Everything in the first two grades is a statement about the mark model: the tape read as stripes and marks, with the rules applied where their patterns match. The model is exact wherever marks stay far enough apart for the patterns to be unambiguous, which is itself checked for every clean tape below 2⁵⁹ and measured beyond; for the certified theorem about the end of the loop the distance needed is known exactly, 447 cells.

Three questions remain, each about an infinite family of coincidences in binary digits: routes deeper than the tree, halts carried by the extra-wall loop alone, and marks touching. Their prices are measured; none can be finished by enumeration.

The theorem about the end of the loop, in full

Setting. A round starts with the gap at cell 1, three walls at a < b < c and the tape ending at E, and the next two rounds have shapes gap-block-wall and gap-marker-block: a phase-0 pass start. Put R = E − c − 1, the end countdown, and L = 147b + 280E − 392c − 578, the left countdown. Write s = log₂(E/R) for how late the run is, λ = log₂(|L|/E) for how large L is, and σ = 3s + 2λ.

Statement. If the right-end run through this state ever brings the gap within 2 cells of the end marker, then at the pass start where the loop was last entered, R is one of 2, 4, 8, 16, 24, 32, 48, 64 or 176 and L lies in the class in the table. Working back through the passes, at any pass start of the run R = 16ᵖ·r for one of those r, with r a multiple of 4 if p ≥ 1, and L ≡ 64ᵖ·cr modulo 64ᵖ·Mr. Because a pass divides R by 16 and L by 64, σ is the same at every pass start of the stretch, and the table's worst row gives log₂E ≤ σ + 6.38. The constant is sharp: for R = 176 and L = −256 there are pass-start tapes that halt, at every width, though none the machine reaches.

Why it holds. From a pass start with R fixed, every way the rules can go is a finite tree once the tests are read as conditions on the binary digits of a, b and E: each test either follows from the digits already fixed or splits the class in two. I followed every branch for every even R up to 4096, in two separate programs. One orders the marks by a concrete far-apart geometry and was rerun in five geometries and with walls brought close together; the other decides every comparison of positions on the cone of large gaps and counts any comparison it cannot decide that way. There were none, in 6.7 million comparisons. Both find the same nine closing values, the same densities, and the same classes. For R above 4096 nothing new can happen: at most 8 end meetings follow a loop state without a new pass, and 8 halvings from more than 4096 cannot reach 2.

How it is certified. The search's result is written out as a list of residue classes of (a, b, E) with the claimed outcome of each, and a separate program, which shares no code with either search, re-derives every outcome from the mark rules on symbolic positions: each digit test must be decided by the class, each comparison of positions by the signs of its gap coefficients alone, and the classes of each R must partition the start class. The checker also records, for every comparison it decides, how far apart the marks must be for the decision to be the true one. The answer is 447 cells: for every pass start whose three stripes exceed that, the theorem holds as stated; a pass start with a shorter stripe is a tape with two marks a few hundred cells apart, which is the third of the three open families below. The same checker on the exit certificate (R free) shows its decisions exact once R + 1 exceeds 241, so the two certificates overlap on 242 ≤ R ≤ 4096 and together cover every R.

In Lean. The statement with the table's classes of L, without the bound 4096, is a theorem about the machine in Lean 4, within the loop: from a phase-0 pass start whose stripes exceed 448 cells, the run closes before it leaves the loop only from a pass start whose R is in the closing set and whose L is in that R's class (one split tree with R a free coordinate for every R ≥ 178, one tree for each smaller R, and induction on the halting time through every pass start the run lands on: after a full pass, a half pass or a chain break at round 7, each with its exact map on E, R and L). The bound below is a Lean theorem as well: a close through the loop needs R³/L² ≤ 176³/256², which is log₂E ≤ σ + 6.38, at the start of the run's last stretch of full passes. A chain break at round 7 can be followed by a close only when E < 2²⁴, so from wider pass starts that is the first pass start, or the one after a single half pass. From E ≥ 2¹³ every landing is again a pass start with stripes over 448 cells, except after a half pass whose middle wall is within 896 cells of the end, so from wider pass starts the run leaves the loop only at a round start that is not three walls. Past that point a tree for each class of pass starts follows the run on to the next landing or a wider clean tape, at every depth with nothing evaluated, so a halt through a pass start needs a closing class, a pass start the tree leaves open at every depth, or a clean tape after it. Some of the open ones are real halts, and that is a theorem as well: one pass start in eight with R = 6 (those with b ≡ 2 mod 8 and W ≡ 2 mod 4) leaves the loop at round 2 and meets the end at distance 0 in round 7, and the machine halts there (from walls 743, 1,802 and 3,319, width 3,326, after 11,362,209 steps). More generally, a close at distance 0 that the mark model reaches is proved to be a halt, 54 steps later: every pass start in the trees that the model takes to a close halts, eight classes by residues of b and W with R in (2, 4, 6, 8, 16, 32, 48).

What it buys. A run that halts on a tape of width 2ᵏ must enter the loop with σ ≥ k − 6.38. Since |L| ≤ 280E, λ ≤ 8.13 and so s ≥ (k − 22.6)/3: the loop must be entered at least that many halvings below the width. Everything seen in the machine's own runs has σ ≤ 32.2; without the extra-wall loop the latest entry any mechanism produces is 36.45 (exact, from the staircase plateau's limit); with pieces of the extra-wall loop the route tree's ceilings reach 52.86 by 62 digits. For the exhaustive frontier, widths up to 2⁶², a halt needs σ ≥ 55.6.

Ways in, worked through

A route is a residue class. Start a segment from a two-marker state with the marker at m and the end at E, both free. Each test the rules make reads a binary digit of a position, which is an affine form in m and E, so the states that follow one sequence of rounds form a coset of a lattice in the (m, E) plane, of index 2 to the power of the digits read. The route tree follows every such coset until the segment ends or reaches a phase-0 pass start. Order comparisons between marks are decided at the asymptotic point, and the tree is the same at m/E = 1/2, 1/64, 63/64 and 5/16, so the branching holds for every ratio.

At the entry, R and L are affine in m and E. Dividing by E, σ depends only on t = m/E, a smooth function on (0, 1) whose maximum is the route's ceiling. The commonest route, gap-marker-marker-marker, then gap-marker-marker-wall, gap-marker-wall-wall, gap-wall-wall-wall, has R = (3E − m + 13)/8 and L = (854E − 343m − 1030)/16, so σ(t) = 3 log₂(8/(3 − t)) + 2 log₂((854 − 343t)/16), which runs from 15.72 to 15.99. By the theorem, no two-marker state entering by this route can halt on a tape wider than 2²²·⁴ cells, whatever its gaps and line-ups; the empirical ceiling of 8 on its gap, found earlier, is a shadow of this.

The deep routes. The highest ceilings come from long lead-ins through pieces of the extra-wall loop. The top entry at 62 digits (ceiling 52.86, a 41-round route with two detour-type passes and one standard pass) enters the plain loop 15.1 halvings below the width; rebuilt as an integer state 2⁶⁴ cells wide it enters with σ = 52.854, as predicted. Its ceiling allows a halt only below 2⁵⁹·²⁴, inside the exhaustive range by 0.76 of a binary digit. At 64 digits one entry crosses the threshold, with ceiling 54.25; its own tests fix R ≡ 12 (mod 16), and no closing value or pass is compatible with that, so it is excluded at every width without any enumeration. The 64-digit tree was rerun with every addition on a position form checked in 128-bit arithmetic, after the first run's deepest paths had overflowed 64-bit integers: the rerun has the same 5,983,365 entries of ceiling ≥ 20 and the same ceilings, and drops 113,555 overflowing paths, 10⁻¹⁴ of the measure, as undecided.

Below the ceiling, the candidates are explicit. On a route's coset, R = 16ᵖ·r is one linear equation, a line of lattice points; L's class thins the line to an arithmetic progression. For every route decided within 46 digits and every width from 2¹⁷ to its lock limit, that progression is empty: 11,305 routes fix R in a class that contains no closing value at all, 7,915 more have L's class inconsistent on every pinned line, and the remaining 213,236 have consistent congruences but no lattice point in the window the lock leaves. The older version of this check had 866,445 looser candidates to run; the exact one has none.

What each computation checks

The class tree over n (the exhaustive check) follows every residue class of n from its clean tape to the next, splitting a class when a test needs another digit, and records every comparison of positions so that each settled class is a proof for all its members. Two engines wrote independently agree at every shared level, and the minima of all separations are logged, which is how contacts are excluded below 2⁵⁹.

The route tree over (m, E) does the same for two-marker states with both numbers free, and records at every phase-0 pass start the forms of R and L. Its results were replayed on the integer engine for the top entries at 40, 58, 62 and 64 digits, and the trees to 36 and 40 digits are certified: every one of their 946,207 and 3,949,190 leaves is written out with its meeting outcomes and round shapes, a separate checker replays each from the start state with its own implementation of the rules, imposing a recorded outcome only where the class does not decide it, and the split sequences of all leaves are verified to form a complete prefix code, which is the partition of the start class. The checker's own count of pass starts agrees with the explorer's in every ceiling bucket.

The endgame search starts from a pass start with R fixed and a, b, E free, and is the finite search behind the theorem. The certificates are its leaves written out (three files: the phase-0 starts, the starts in either phase, and the loop state with R free), and the checkers are two short programs that accept a certificate only if every leaf follows from the rules and the leaves partition the start class; all three are accepted with no failure, no undecided digit and no uncovered configuration. What a reader of the checkers has to trust is the rule table under "How the rules give the round recurrence", exact rational arithmetic, and the bookkeeping itself: that a class on which every digit test is decided and every comparison has a fixed sign follows one sequence of rules. The Lean formalization re-proves the mark rules inside Lean 4 from a definition of the machine: a rule is a theorem about `steps n` on a configuration whose surroundings are variables, single steps are checked by evaluation, repeats are crossed by the three cycle lemmas, and the file of 21 rules is generated from the Python prover's traces, so the two proofs are the same proof in two checkers. On top of the rules it proves the round theorem (from the tape of any well-formed mark state the machine reaches the next round start the mark model computes, or halts where the model says halt; from blank tape it follows the model for as many rounds as the model covers), and it runs the model on symbolic positions with every decision checked, proved sound against the concrete model: every leaf of the endgame, any-phase and exit certificates checks through it, which makes each non-closing leaf a statement about the machine. Re-splitting the start classes by coordinates in Lean gives coverage by construction, and two results are proved as theorems about the machine, each run followed to a stated end (its end meetings break the halving chain, the tape is clean, the run leaves phase 0, or it reaches the next pass start): every loop state with large gaps runs to such an end without halting or closing, and so does every phase-0 pass start unless its R is in the closing set and its L in R's class (the tree checks are evaluated by the Lean kernel). Two more theorems carry this to every width and to the halt itself: by induction over the passes, a run through the loop closes only from a pass start whose R is in the closing set and whose L is in its class, and since each pass divides R by 16 and L by 64, only if log₂E ≤ σ + 6.38 at its first pass start; and a halt needs a contact, a close at the right end unless an interior contact comes first. The route tree, at every depth, and the pass maps of the extra-wall loop are theorems in Lean too, and an end-to-end theorem puts the pieces together from blank tape: a halt needs a clean tape of width at least 2⁴¹ that the clean-tape tree leaves open at every depth. From width 2²⁰ the same statement is checked by the Lean kernel alone, with no compiled evaluation. Comparing the proved model with the simulator used by the checkers and explorers found one slip in the simulator (the trailing wall: a shift reaching a block that ends the tape); it is fixed, a differential test checks every simulator against the proved model, and the certificates and the exhaustive check to 54 digits give the same results with the fixed programs. The candidate solver turns a route's forms and the closing table into explicit lattice points and was tested against brute force. The invariant search looked for any conserved quantity with weights on patterns of up to 7 cells and positions modulo up to 8, modulo 2, 3, 5, 7 and over the rationals: the count of 1s mod 3 is the only one, so no conservation law separates the halting configurations from the reachable ones.

What none of them can do is finish an infinite family. Beyond the frontier a halt needs a route about 1.2k digits deep at width 2ᵏ, more digits than n has, and then about 2.5(k − s) further binary digits of agreement. Each such requirement is a statement about the binary digits of particular whole numbers, which is where the question rests.

Where the steps go

In the first 3 billion steps, 66.4% are carry steps and 33.2% are pass steps. The last 0.4% are turns (0.18%), gap steps (0.10%), other fixed sequences (0.09%) and shifts (0.03%).
