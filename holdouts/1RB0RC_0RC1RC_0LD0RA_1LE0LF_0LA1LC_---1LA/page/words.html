Look at the tape each time the head, in state E, reaches the left end of the visited tape: an event. The tape is then a block of seven-cell words 1011001, a short irregular zone, and then blocks of the three-cell word 011 separated by a few zeros. The last block, (011)^r, ends the tape.

Going right, the head shifts a block of three-cell words by one cell (3 steps a word). Going left, it crosses two words at a time (18 steps). So what happens at the end of a block depends only on whether its length is even or odd: every cycle rule the proof needed has the form "for all lengths of this parity".

The Lean proof works at this level with a symbolic executor: a program whose tape is a list of cells and blocks (w)^n, where n may be c + a x with x unknown. One move of the executor either crosses a whole block with a shift rule, or makes one ordinary step (opening a block only when its count is surely at least 1). A shift rule says: in state q, with the word u just behind the head, the head crosses k copies of a word w and leaves the word v behind it, in s steps, without looking at anything else. Each of the 31 shift rules is a Lean statement about the machine for every tape around those cells, checked by the kernel (shRTbl_ok, shLTbl_ok in lean/ExecData.lean), and the soundness theorem run_sound (lean/Exec.lean, kernel) says that whatever the executor does in some number of moves, the machine does, for every value of the unknowns. That is the theorem that makes level 2 answer the same as level 1.

The browser writes u, then n crossings' worth of copies of w, between twelve random cells on each side, puts the head in state q where the rule says, and runs the real machine for n·s steps. The rule holds if the head never reads a random cell, ends in state q at the far side, and the cells read u vn as the rule says. Words are written nearest cell first, as in the Lean file, so on the left of the head they appear reversed.
